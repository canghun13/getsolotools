Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in Oregon

July 18, 2026 · Updated Nov 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Oregon is one of the few states where attorneys generally can't represent either side in small claims court without a judge's permission — a rule built specifically to keep the process accessible to freelancers who file their own cases. Here's what Oregon freelancers need to know about late fees, from the default interest rate to collecting through the Circuit Court Small Claims Department.

Oregon's 9% Default Rate

Under ORS 82.010(1), Oregon's default interest rate is 9% per year whenever the parties haven't agreed to a rate — this applies to overdue accounts and unpaid contract balances alike. Most Oregon freelancers still write their own rate directly into contracts and invoices, typically 1.5% per month (18% annually), which is double the statutory default and enforceable as the agreed rate as long as it's disclosed and reasonable.

Oregon's loan cap, and when it can bite: ORS 82.010(3) limits interest on a loan of $50,000 or less — business loans included — to the greater of 12% a year or 5 points above the Federal Reserve's 90-day commercial paper discount rate, and a lender who goes over it forfeits all interest (ORS 82.010(4)). An ordinary overdue service invoice isn't a loan, which is why 1.5%-per-month late charges are common in Oregon. But if you turn an overdue balance into a written payment plan or promissory note, you're arguably now lending — keep that rate at 12% a year or less.

Oregon's Hidden Leverage: Attorney Fees on Claims of $10,000 or Less

Most states make each side pay its own lawyer unless the contract says otherwise. Oregon doesn't. Under ORS 20.082, when you sue on a contract for $10,000 or less (principal plus interest) and the contract has no attorney fee clause, the court awards reasonable attorney fees to the prevailing party. To claim them as the plaintiff, you must send the client a written demand for payment at least 20 days before filing suit, and you lose the right if the client tendered the full amount before you sued. The rule cuts both ways — a client who wins gets fees too — so use it when your invoice is solid. A clear demand letter that cites ORS 20.082 often gets a slow-paying client to pay before it ever reaches court.

The Attorney Restriction That Sets Oregon Apart

Oregon's Small Claims Department keeps lawyers out by default: under ORS 46.415, only the parties and their witnesses may appear without the judge's consent, and a business can appear without an attorney. Many states allow attorneys freely in small claims court, while some, like California, bar them entirely. Oregon's approach — attorneys only with leave of court — is meant to keep the forum genuinely accessible to freelancers who can't afford legal representation, while still letting a judge allow it in unusual cases. If you're an Oregon freelancer filing your own small claims case, expect to be facing the other party directly rather than their lawyer.

Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in Oregon

  • Put your rate in writing. "Invoices unpaid after 15 days accrue interest at 1.5% per month" beats the 9% statutory default and is clear evidence of what was agreed.
  • Reference the rate on every invoice so the client sees it without needing to dig up the original contract.
  • Know the mandatory threshold. Oregon requires that claims of $750 or less be filed in the Small Claims Department (ORS 46.405) — you can't choose regular Circuit Court for very small amounts.
  • Track your filing window. Written contract claims must be filed within 6 years (ORS 12.080), giving you a reasonably long runway to collect.

Know the jury-trial exit. If your claim (including interest, fees and costs) is more than $750, the defendant has a constitutional right to move the case out of small claims by requesting a jury trial in writing (ORS 46.455). If that happens, your ORS 20.082 demand letter matters even more, because the case becomes a regular civil action.

Collecting in Oregon

Oregon's Circuit Court Small Claims Department handles money disputes up to $10,000 (ORS 46.405). Claims of $750 or less must be filed there; claims between $750 and $10,000 may go to Small Claims or the regular Circuit Court civil docket. Filing fees are modest and depend on the amount claimed; check your county circuit court's current fee schedule.

  • Oregon Small Claims Department: up to $10,000 — attorneys need the judge's permission to appear (ORS 46.415)
  • Written contract statute of limitations: 6 years (ORS 12.080(1))
  • Post-judgment interest: 9% per year, or the contract rate if it's higher (ORS 82.010(2))
  • Small claims judgments are final — no appeal may be taken (ORS 46.485(4)) — so bring every document the first time

What late fee rate is enforceable in Oregon? +

Freelancers can write a different rate into their contract — commonly 1.5% per month (18% annually) on overdue service invoices. Can I hire a lawyer for a small claims case in Oregon? +

Only with the judge's permission. Under ORS 46.415, attorneys may not appear in Oregon's Small Claims Department without the judge's consent, and a business can appear without one. It's designed to keep the process accessible to freelancers representing themselves.

Can I recover attorney fees for an unpaid invoice in Oregon? +

Often, yes. Under ORS 20.082, if you sue on a contract for $10,000 or less and the contract has no attorney fee clause, the prevailing party gets reasonable attorney fees — but as plaintiff you must make a written demand for payment at least 20 days before filing. The rule also protects a client who wins.

What are the late fee laws for invoices in Oregon? +

No Oregon statute caps a late charge on an ordinary business invoice; the rate in your signed contract controls, and 1.5% per month is common. With no agreed rate, money owed earns 9% a year (ORS 82.010(1)). Loans and payment plans of $50,000 or less are capped at the greater of 12% or 5 points over the Fed discount rate.
