Late Fee Laws for Freelancers & Small Businesses in Alaska

November 23, 2026 · 7 min read

Alaska gives an overdue invoice one of the highest no-contract defaults in the country — 10.5% a year — but it also caps what you can write into a smaller contract, and it gives you only three years to sue. In regular civil cases, the losing side usually pays part of the winner's attorney fees. Here's what Alaska freelancers need to know.

No Rate in the Contract? Alaska Applies 10.5%

AS 45.45.010(a) sets the rate at 10.5% a year on money after it is due when no other rate was agreed. For a freelancer with a silent contract, that's a strong fallback — higher than every lower-48 state except a handful.

The Agreed-Rate Cap — and the $25,000 Exemption

Under AS 45.45.010(b), a contract can't set a rate above the greater of 10% a year or 5 percentage points above the rate the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (12th District) charges member banks for advances on the date of the agreement. With that advance rate in the low-to-mid single digits, the cap usually works out to about 10% a year — so the national 1.5%-per-month habit (18%) is over the line on a typical freelance invoice. The cap doesn't apply to a contract whose principal exceeds $25,000.

Overcharging is costly: if usury is established, the court enters judgment for the principal only (AS 45.45.040), and a borrower who paid excess interest can recover double the interest paid (AS 45.45.030). Write about 0.8% a month on contracts of $25,000 or less, and check the current Federal Reserve advance rate if you want to go higher.

Only Three Years to Sue

Alaska's statute of limitations for contract claims is 3 years (AS 09.10.053) — one of the shortest in the country. Don't let an unpaid invoice drift into its second year without a written demand and a decision about filing.

Small Claims: $10,000 — and Rule 82 Attorney Fees

Alaska's District and Magistrate Courts hear small claims up to $10,000; a larger claim can still use small claims if you give up the excess. A losing party can appeal, generally within 30 days of the clerk's distribution of the judgment. In regular civil cases, Alaska Civil Rule 82 awards the prevailing party a share of attorney fees on a set schedule — for a money judgment, 20% of the first $25,000 after a contested trial, 18% if contested without trial, and 10% if uncontested. That risk runs both ways, so it's a strong reason for a client to settle a solid invoice.

Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in Alaska

  • Write about 0.8% a month on contracts of $25,000 or less, or check the current Fed advance rate before going higher.
  • For contracts over $25,000, the cap doesn't apply — 1% to 1.5% a month is common.
  • Put the rate and due date on every invoice, and keep the signed agreement.
  • Calendar the 3-year deadline from the invoice due date.

Collecting in Alaska

  • Small claims (District or Magistrate Court): up to $10,000; appeal generally within 30 days
  • Default interest with no agreed rate: 10.5% a year (AS 45.45.010(a))
  • Agreed-rate cap: greater of 10% or Fed advance rate + 5, unless principal exceeds $25,000 (AS 45.45.010(b))
  • Statute of limitations: 3 years on a contract (AS 09.10.053)
  • Regular civil cases: partial attorney fees to the winner under Civil Rule 82

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the maximum late fee interest in Alaska? +

For a contract with principal of $25,000 or less, the greater of 10% a year or 5 points above the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco's advance rate on the agreement date (AS 45.45.010(b)) — usually about 10%. Contracts over $25,000 are exempt from the cap.

What interest applies in Alaska if my contract doesn't state a rate? +

10.5% a year on money after it becomes due (AS 45.45.010(a)).

How long do I have to sue over an unpaid invoice in Alaska? +

Three years on a contract claim (AS 09.10.053) — shorter than most states, so act early.

Can I recover attorney fees in Alaska? +

In regular civil cases, Alaska Civil Rule 82 awards the prevailing party a percentage of attorney fees based on the judgment — 20% of the first $25,000 after a contested trial, less if the case ends earlier. The losing side bears it, which applies to you too if you lose.
