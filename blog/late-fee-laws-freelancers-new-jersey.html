Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in New Jersey

May 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 12, 2026 · 7 min read

If you've read that New Jersey has a "Freelance Worker Protection Act" with written-contract rules and double damages, you're not alone — several guides say so, and an earlier version of this page did too. It isn't law. As of October 2026, New Jersey has no statewide freelance payment protection statute. What protects a New Jersey freelancer is the contract, the 6% default interest rate, and a small claims court that now goes to $5,000. Here's what actually applies.

Does New Jersey Have a Freelance Isn't Free Law? Not Yet

New Jersey has considered one for years. Assembly Bill A1526 (2018–2019 session) would have required written contracts and timely payment for freelancers paid $600 or more, with liquidated damages and a complaint process — but it was pocket-vetoed in January 2020 when the governor didn't act before the session ended. Versions have circulated in Trenton since without becoming law.

  • There's no statutory written-contract requirement for freelance work in New Jersey and no statutory 30-day payment deadline.
  • There's no double-damages remedy for a late-paying client. Don't cite one in a demand letter — a client's lawyer will know it doesn't exist, and it undermines everything else in your letter.
  • New Jersey's recent labor activity has gone the other way: the state's labor department moved forward with stricter independent-contractor classification rules in 2026. Those decide whether you're a contractor at all — they don't speed up payment.

Where a freelance payment law may still help: if your client is in New York, New York's statewide Freelance Isn't Free Act may cover the engagement, and Illinois and California have their own versions. Our Freelance Isn't Free Act state guide compares them.

What Actually Protects You: Your Contract

Without a freelance statute, your late fee exists only if it's in the agreement. New Jersey law doesn't cap commercial late fee rates, so you can specify the rate you and your client agree to. The standard 1.5% per month (18% annually) is widely used — our Late Payment Fee Calculator shows the running total at any rate.

New Jersey's default interest rate: Under N.J.S.A. 31:1-1, the legal rate of interest is 6% per year when no rate is specified. If your contract doesn't name a late fee rate, 6% annually is what a court would typically apply — a third of the 18% most freelancers charge. Always specify your rate in writing.

Pennsylvania Freelancers Charge 1.5% a Month — Does New Jersey Have a Similar Statute?

This question comes up a lot along the Delaware River, and the answer is the same on both sides: neither state has a statute that sets 1.5% a month for freelance invoices. The 1.5% figure is a contract convention, not a legal entitlement — in Pennsylvania as in New Jersey, it's enforceable because the client agreed to it in writing. Without that clause, both states fall back to a 6% default. If you work for clients on both sides of the river, use the same written clause for both and see the Pennsylvania guide for the differences in court limits.

If a New Jersey Client Doesn't Pay

  • Send a dated written demand stating the invoice number, amount, original due date and your contract's late fee clause. Most disputes end here.
  • Small Claims Section of the Special Civil Part: claims of $5,000 or less, designed for people without a lawyer.
  • Special Civil Part: claims up to $20,000, with a somewhat more formal process.
  • Superior Court: above $20,000.

Older guides still show a $3,000 small claims limit; the New Jersey courts' own guidance puts it at $5,000. Whether a claim is worth your time is a separate question — is it worth suing a client over an unpaid invoice works through the arithmetic.

Practical Tips for New Jersey Freelancers

  • Use a written contract on every project — with no freelance statute, it's the only thing that sets your payment terms
  • Include your late fee rate in the contract (the NJ default of 6% annually is too low for most freelancers)
  • Put the due date and late fee on every invoice, not only in the contract
  • If the client is in New York, check whether New York's Freelance Isn't Free Act covers the work
  • Keep claims of $5,000 or less in the Small Claims Section, where you don't need a lawyer
  • Document everything: signed contracts, delivered work, payment deadlines, all client communications

New Jersey doesn't give freelancers a statutory shortcut, which makes the contract do all the work. Put your rate, due date and late fee in writing before you start, use the $5,000 small claims track for most unpaid invoices, and don't rely on a freelance protection law that hasn't been passed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What interest rate applies if my New Jersey contract doesn't specify one? +

New Jersey's default legal rate is 6% per year under N.J.S.A. 31:1-1, far below the 18% (1.5% per month) most freelancers charge. Always specify your own rate in writing — courts will enforce the agreed rate instead of falling back to the 6% default.

How does New Jersey's small claims limit compare to nearby states? +

New Jersey's Special Civil Part Small Claims handles disputes of $5,000 or less — the same as Maryland, below Pennsylvania's $12,000 and New York City's $10,000. For claims up to $20,000, the regular Special Civil Part still offers a simpler process before Superior Court becomes necessary.

Does New Jersey have a Freelance Isn't Free Act or Freelance Worker Protection Act? +

No. As of October 2026 New Jersey has no statewide freelance payment protection law. Assembly Bill A1526 (2018–2019) would have required written contracts and timely payment for freelancers, but it was pocket-vetoed in January 2020, and later versions have not been enacted. There is no statutory double-damages remedy for late payment in New Jersey, so your contract's late fee clause is what protects you.

Pennsylvania freelancers charge 1.5% a month. Does New Jersey have a similar statute? +

Neither state has a statute that sets 1.5% a month for freelance invoices. It is a contract convention that is enforceable because the client agreed to it in writing. Without a written rate, New Jersey falls back to 6% a year under N.J.S.A. 31:1-1.
