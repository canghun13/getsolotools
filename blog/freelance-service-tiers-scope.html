Basic, Standard, Premium: How to Draw the Scope Lines Between Freelance Tiers

August 31, 2026 · 10 min read

There is plenty written about why three tiers work — anchoring, the Goldilocks effect, the middle option winning both comparisons. That part is settled and you can read it anywhere.

What almost nobody writes about is the part that actually breaks in practice: where the scope lines go. Tiers fail not because the prices were wrong but because Standard and Premium weren't distinguishable enough for a client to see, or because the boundary between them was never written down, so every Standard client asked for Premium work and got it.

The Rule: Tiers Differ by Deliverable, Not by Effort

The most common way tiers collapse is defining them by how much work you'll do. "Premium includes more in-depth research" is invisible to a buyer — they can't verify it, can't compare it, and can't tell what they're paying extra for. Clients default to the cheapest tier whenever the difference isn't legible.

Every tier boundary should be something a client could count or check. There are four levers that do this reliably:

What Each Tier Is Actually For

Basic — the smallest version that still works

A client who buys only Basic should get something usable, not a teaser that forces an upgrade. Deliberately crippled entry tiers generate refund requests and bad reviews, and they don't convert to upgrades as often as people expect. Basic's job is to remove the "is this person any good" hesitation at low risk.

Standard — the one you actually want sold

Standard should be Basic plus the two or three things clients ask for most. If you already know what the common add-on requests are, that list is your Standard tier. This is the tier to design first and the one your pricing should be built around, because it's where most of the volume lands.

Premium — done-for-you, with a defined ceiling

Widest scope, fastest turnaround, most support. But "most" must still be a number. More on this below, because it's where the money leaks.

The Trap: Premium That Pays Worse Than Basic

Divide every tier's price by the hours it honestly takes — including revisions and admin — before you publish it. Freelancers routinely discover that their proudest package pays a lower effective hourly rate than their cheapest one, because Premium accumulated unlimited revisions, extra calls, and open-ended support while the price only went up by 80%.

When that happens, every sale of your best tier quietly costs you money, and the better you market it the worse your year gets.

Two rules prevent it. First, cap revisions at every tier, Premium included. Unlimited revisions at the top is the fastest way to invert your ladder, because the clients who buy Premium tend to be the ones with the most opinions and the most stakeholders. If you want Premium to feel generous, widen the revision window or speed up each round rather than uncapping the count.

Second, price the top tier on turnaround and support, not just volume. Rush delivery is worth real money to a client and costs you scheduling rather than scope. Writing the Boundary So It Holds

A tier table on your website is marketing. What stops scope drift is the same table restated as a scope of work, attached to the agreement, with exclusions written out. Our guide to writing a scope of work covers why exclusions matter as much as deliverables — with tiers, that's doubly true, because the excluded items are literally the next tier up.

Tier scope wording This engagement is the [Standard] package as described below. Deliverables: [list, with counts]. Revisions: [N] rounds, each requested within [X] business days of delivery. Turnaround: [N] business days from receipt of all client materials. Not included in this package: [list the items that belong to the tier above]. Any item listed as not included may be added by written change order at the rates in the Package Schedule.

That last sentence does most of the work. It converts an upgrade request from a negotiation into an already-priced menu item. Generate the full document with the scope of work generator, and if you're sending tiers as options to a prospect, the proposal generator handles the presentation.

When a Standard Client Asks for Premium Work

This will happen on nearly every project, and how you handle the first request decides the rest of the engagement.

The instinct is to absorb it — it's small, you don't want friction, and the relationship feels worth more than the item. But absorbing the first request teaches the client that the tier lines are decorative, and it guarantees a second one. The alternative isn't confrontation. It's naming the item and offering the choice:

The response Happy to do that — [item] sits in the Premium package rather than Standard, so it's [$X] to add. Want me to include it, or keep the current scope and revisit after launch?

Four things make this work: it says yes rather than no, it points at a boundary that already existed before the project started, it gives a specific price instead of "that'll cost extra," and it offers a face-saving exit. Most clients pick one and move on without any awkwardness at all.

If requests keep arriving despite this, the underlying issue is usually scope definition rather than the client — see the contract clauses worth redlining for the change-order language that backs it up.

When Tiers Are the Wrong Model

Tiers suit repeatable work where the deliverable is roughly the same shape each time: logo packages, websites of a known size, monthly content, recurring maintenance. They save scoping time and let buyers choose without a call.

They're a poor fit when:

  • Discovery changes the shape of the job. If you can't know the deliverable until you've dug in, a tier is a guess you're contractually bound to.
  • You're pricing on outcome value rather than deliverable count — a tier table anchors the conversation to units and away from results.
  • The work is genuinely bespoke. Publishing tiers you constantly deviate from is worse than not publishing them.

A common resolution is running both: tiers for the standard offering, and custom quoting above the top tier. Saying "projects beyond Premium are quoted individually" costs nothing and keeps the ladder honest. Frequently Asked Questions

What should go in a Basic, Standard and Premium freelance package?

Basic is the smallest version of the job that still works on its own — a client who buys only Basic should get a usable result, not a teaser. Standard is Basic plus the additions clients ask for most often, which is why it's normally the tier you actually want people to buy. Premium is the done-for-you version: widest scope, fastest turnaround, most revisions, and usually some form of ongoing support. The mistake to avoid is defining tiers by effort rather than by deliverable. If the only difference between Standard and Premium is that Premium takes you longer, clients can't see the difference and will default to the cheapest option.

How many revisions should each tier include?

A common structure is one round at Basic, two or three at Standard, and a defined higher number at Premium — but the important part is that the number is finite at every tier, including Premium. Unlimited revisions at the top tier is the single most reliable way to make your best-paying package your worst-paying work, because the clients who buy it are the ones with the most opinions. If you want Premium to feel generous, use a longer revision window or faster turnaround on each round rather than an uncapped count.

How should I price three tiers relative to each other?

Set the middle tier first, because that's the one most clients choose and it should reflect the work you actually want. From there a common spacing is Basic at roughly 50–60% of Standard and Premium at roughly 175–200% of Standard. The spacing matters less than the check most people skip: divide each tier's price by the hours it honestly takes, including revisions and admin. If Premium works out to a lower hourly rate than Basic, the ladder is upside down and every sale of your best package quietly costs you money.

What do I say when a Standard client asks for Premium work?

Treat it as a change order rather than a favour or a confrontation. Name the item, say which tier it belongs to, give the price to add it, and offer the choice: add it now at that price, or keep the current scope. This works because the tier document already said so before the project started, so you're pointing at an agreed boundary rather than inventing one under pressure. The failure mode is absorbing the first request silently, which teaches the client that tier lines are decorative and guarantees a second request.

Is tiered pricing better than custom quotes for freelancers?

Tiers work best for repeatable work where the deliverable is roughly the same shape every time — logo packages, website builds of a known size, monthly content, recurring maintenance. They save you scoping time and let buyers self-select without a sales call. Custom quoting stays better for genuinely bespoke projects, work where discovery changes the shape of the job, and anything priced on the value of the outcome rather than the deliverable. Many freelancers run both: tiers for the standard offering, custom quotes above the top tier.

The prices are the easy part. Write down what each tier does and doesn't include, cap the revisions everywhere, and check that your best package isn't your worst-paid one — and the ladder holds on its own.
