Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in Ohio

May 27, 2026 · Updated Nov 2, 2026 · 8 min read

Ohio has no statute written specifically about late fees on commercial invoices, so a freelancer's rate comes from the contract — but only a contract the client actually agreed to. Ohio's Supreme Court has held that a rate printed only on your invoices doesn't count, and with no agreed rate you get Ohio's statutory rate, 7% a year in 2026. Freelancers working in Columbus also have a city ordinance on their side. Here's what Ohio freelancers need to know.

Ohio's Approach to Commercial Late Fees

Unlike some states that specify maximum late fee rates in their statutes, Ohio leaves commercial payment terms largely to the parties involved. For business-to-business transactions — which covers most freelance work — there's no statute setting a late-charge ceiling. Courts apply a general reasonableness standard: a fee that's disproportionate to the original invoice amount or that functions as a penalty rather than a compensation for delay may not be fully enforced.

In practice, 1.5% per month (18% annually) is the common written rate for commercial accounts in Ohio. Some businesses write in up to 2% per month, but no Ohio statute or court decision blesses a particular number, so 1.5% is the safer line for a freelancer who may end up explaining the rate to a small claims magistrate.

What matters more than the number is whether the client agreed to it. In Minster Farmers Coop. Exchange Co. v. Meyer, 2008-Ohio-1259, the Ohio Supreme Court held that an interest rate printed on invoices and account statements is not a "written contract" — the rate has to appear in a writing both parties assented to, such as a signed contract or accepted terms. Without that, the client owes interest only at the statutory rate under ORC 1343.03(A), which the Tax Commissioner sets each year under ORC 5703.47: 7% per year for 2026.

Ohio's usury law context: ORC 1343.01 limits interest in a written instrument for the payment of money to 8% per year, but it exempts instruments payable on demand or in a single installment and loans to businesses (ORC 1343.01(B)(5)–(6)). A freelance invoice due in one payment generally falls outside the 8% limit, which is why written rates of 1.5% per month are common in Ohio. If you bill a client in scheduled installments under a payment plan, check the exemptions before writing in a high rate.

Ohio's Prompt Payment Act for Government Clients

If you do work for Ohio state agencies, ORC 126.30 requires them to pay a proper invoice within 30 days of receipt. If payment is late, the agency owes interest at the same statutory rate set annually under ORC 5703.47 — 7% per year for 2026. Counties, cities and school districts are not state agencies under this section; check their own contract terms.

This statutory interest applies automatically — you don't need a late fee clause in your contract to be entitled to it. If you're invoicing a state agency in Ohio and payment is dragging past 30 days, you're already earning interest on that balance by law.

Private clients are not covered by the Prompt Payment Act. Your rights with a private client depend entirely on your contract.

Does Ohio Have a Freelancer Protection Law?

Not statewide. As of 2026, Ohio has no law comparable to New York's Freelance Isn't Free Act or Illinois's Freelance Worker Protection Act. But Columbus does: its Freelance Worker Protections, in effect since May 31, 2023, cover a freelancer hired by a hiring party in Columbus for work worth $250 or more over four months. For those jobs, the city requires:

  • A written contract that itemizes the services, the rate or method of pay, and the payment date (or how the date will be set).
  • Payment on time — by the date in the contract, or within 30 days of completing the work if the contract doesn't set one.
  • No retaliation for asserting these rights.

Complaints go to the city's wage theft prevention and enforcement office (Columbus City Code Chapter 377). The ordinance doesn't impose the double damages New York or Illinois allow — the city's role is investigation and connecting freelancers with legal help — so the contract it requires is the real value: it gives you the signed writing Ohio courts look for. Cleveland, Cincinnati and other Ohio cities have no comparable ordinance.

What this means in practice: In states with freelancer protection laws, violations can result in double damages and attorney fee awards — which creates strong deterrence against non-payment. Outside those states, Ohio freelancers — including those covered by the Columbus ordinance — are generally limited to the amount owed plus contractual late fees or statutory interest. This makes a solid written contract your primary enforcement tool.

Ohio is still a contract-friendly state. Well-documented commercial disputes typically resolve in the freelancer's favor in small claims court, particularly when there's a clear written agreement. The lack of a statutory backstop just means you need to do more of the protection work yourself — through your contract terms.

How to Set Up Enforceable Late Fees in Ohio

The requirements for an enforceable late fee in Ohio are straightforward, but getting each element right matters:

  • Written agreement. Your late fee must be specified in writing before work begins — either in your contract or in your standard terms referenced by the contract. An oral agreement about late fees is very difficult to enforce.
  • Specific rate and trigger. State exactly what rate applies and when it starts accruing. "Late fees may apply" is not sufficient. "A late fee of 1.5% per month will accrue on any invoice unpaid after 30 days from the invoice date" is enforceable.
  • Invoice reinforcement. Repeat the late fee terms on every invoice. Accounts payable departments process payments independently — if the terms aren't on the invoice, they may not be aware of them.
  • Written notice when fees begin. Send an email confirming that the invoice is overdue and that late fees are now accruing. This creates a paper trail and often prompts payment without escalation.
  • Updated invoice with fees added. Issue a revised invoice showing the original amount, the late fee calculation, and the updated total. Keep the format professional — late fee invoices that look like debt collection notices can damage the client relationship unnecessarily.

Example late fee clause language

Here's language you can adapt for your contracts:

"Invoices are due within [14/30] days of the invoice date. Any invoice remaining unpaid after the due date will accrue a late fee of 1.5% per month (18% annually) on the outstanding balance. Late fees begin accruing on the first day after the due date and continue until the full balance is received."

Collecting Late Payments in Ohio

Ohio Small Claims Court handles disputes up to $6,000 in Municipal Court. For amounts between $6,000 and $15,000, Municipal Court hears the case on its regular civil docket. Amounts above $15,000 go to Common Pleas Court, where you'll likely want an attorney.

For small claims, you don't need a lawyer, and filing fees are modest. Bring your contract, all invoices, every written communication about the overdue balance, and documentation of when work was completed and delivered. Ohio small claims judges are familiar with business payment disputes, and a well-documented case with a clear written contract typically resolves in the freelancer's favor.

What to bring to small claims court

  • Signed contract (or email chain documenting agreement)
  • Original invoice with your late fee terms visible
  • Updated invoice showing late fees accrued
  • Documentation of work completed and delivered
  • All written communications about the overdue payment
  • Any partial payments received (to show the remaining balance)

Court tiers in Ohio

  • Small Claims: Up to $6,000 — your county Municipal Court, no attorney needed
  • Municipal Court: $6,000–$15,000 — regular civil docket
  • Common Pleas: Over $15,000 — consider an attorney

Practical Tips for Ohio Freelancers

  • Include a specific late fee clause in every signed contract — a rate that appears only on invoices won't bind the client in Ohio
  • 1.5% per month is the safe, standard written rate; without an agreed rate you're limited to the 7% statutory rate (2026)
  • State agencies must pay within 30 days under ORC 126.30, with interest at the statutory rate if they don't — no fee clause needed
  • Follow up overdue invoices in writing within a week of the due date; early written follow-up significantly improves collection rates and creates the paper trail you need
  • Ohio Small Claims Court (up to $6,000) is accessible and doesn't require a lawyer — don't hesitate to use it for clear-cut non-payment cases
  • Document everything: completed work, delivery confirmation, all client communications about payment
  • Consider pairing your invoice with a written contract that makes your payment terms unambiguous from day one

Outside Columbus, Ohio's lack of a dedicated freelancer protection law means your contract is doing a lot of the heavy lifting. The good news is that Ohio courts are generally business-friendly and will enforce well-written commercial contracts. Put your terms in writing, be consistent about stating them on every invoice, and follow up promptly when payment is late — that combination handles most situations before they escalate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What late fee rate is enforceable in Ohio? +

1.5% per month (18% annually) is the common written rate for commercial invoices in Ohio, and a single-payment invoice generally falls outside the 8% limit in ORC 1343.01. The rate must be in a contract or terms both sides agreed to: under Minster Farmers v. Meyer (2008), a rate printed only on invoices doesn't bind the client, who then owes the statutory rate (7% per year for 2026).

How does Ohio's small claims limit compare to nearby states? +

Ohio's Small Claims Court caps disputes at $6,000 — lower than neighboring Indiana's $10,000 and Michigan's $7,000. For claims between $6,000 and $15,000, Ohio Municipal Court still offers a simplified procedure; only amounts above $15,000 require Common Pleas Court, where hiring an attorney becomes more worthwhile.

Does Columbus, Ohio have a freelancer protection law? +

Yes. Since May 31, 2023, Columbus requires a written contract (itemized services, rate, payment date) for freelance work worth $250 or more over four months, payment by the contract date or within 30 days of completion, and no retaliation. Complaints go to the city's wage theft prevention office; the ordinance does not add double damages. No other Ohio city has a comparable ordinance.

What interest can I charge in Ohio if my contract has no late fee clause? +

Ohio's statutory rate under ORC 1343.03(A), set each year under ORC 5703.47 — 7% per year for 2026 — on money due under a contract. A late fee rate that appears only on your invoices doesn't change that; the Ohio Supreme Court requires a writing both parties agreed to.
