Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in Florida

May 19, 2026 · Updated Nov 2, 2026 · 6 min read

Florida has no law written specifically about late fees on business invoices, but it does have a firm interest ceiling: its usury statute makes it unlawful to charge more than the equivalent of 18% per year simple interest on a loan, a forbearance to collect a debt, "or other obligation" (Fla. Stat. § 687.03). That puts the common 1.5%-per-month freelance rate right at the line, not comfortably under it. A separate flat late charge is a different question, and courts also test any fee for reasonableness. Here's how to set up late fees that hold up in Florida.

Does Florida Have a Late Fee Cap for Freelancers?

Yes, for interest. Florida's general usury statute covers far more than bank loans: § 687.03(1) applies to "any loan, advance of money, line of credit, forbearance to enforce the collection of any sum of money, or other obligation," and sets the limit at the equivalent of 18% per year simple interest. Charging more than 25% per year on an extension of credit can be a crime under § 687.071. Whether a particular unpaid service invoice counts as an "other obligation" can be argued, but there is no reason to test it: a written rate of 1.5% per month or less stays inside the line either way.

Residential leases follow their own rules and aren't the model for business invoices. For a B2B freelance invoice, the usury line governs the interest rate, and a court will still ask whether any flat charge is a reasonable estimate of the cost of late payment rather than a penalty.

Rule of thumb: 1.5% per month (18% per year, simple, not compounded) is the most you should write in for Florida interest. Don't compound it monthly — 1.5% compounded works out to about 19.6% a year, which crosses the line. Run the numbers instantly with our Late Payment Fee Calculator.

Can You Add a 5% or $25 Late Charge in Florida?

Usually yes, if the contract says so. A flat late charge (say 5% of the invoice once, or $25) compensates for the cost of chasing the payment; interest compensates for the time the money is outstanding. A Florida collections attorney notes that a 5% late charge can be collected in addition to 18% interest when both are in the agreement. Two cautions: keep the flat charge modest relative to the invoice (a $25 fee on a $150 bookkeeping invoice is about 17%, which invites a penalty argument), and charge it once rather than every month. If you want to stack both, see charging a flat late fee and interest together.

With no rate written into the contract at all, Florida's statutory interest rate applies to money that is due. The state's Chief Financial Officer resets that rate every quarter under Fla. Stat. § 55.03; it is 7.87% per year from October 1, 2026, down from 8.44% in January. The same rate applies to judgments.

What Rate Should Florida Freelancers Charge?

The standard freelance late fee rate is 1.5% per month on the outstanding balance, which works out to 18% per year. This rate is widely recognized, easy to explain to clients, and at the top of what Florida's 18% simple-interest ceiling allows, so don't go above it.

Some freelancers prefer a flat fee — typically $25 to $50 per late invoice — especially on smaller projects where a percentage would amount to just a few dollars. Either approach works if it's clearly stated in writing before work begins; on very small invoices, keep the flat amount proportionate.

Florida also has no legally mandated grace period for commercial invoices. However, offering 5–7 days before fees kick in is standard practice and reduces friction with clients who are slow but not intentionally withholding payment.

No Freelance Protection Law — What That Means

Unlike New York, California, and Illinois, Florida does not have a statewide freelance worker protection law, and no Florida city has adopted one either. There's no law that automatically entitles you to double damages or attorney's fees if a client pays late. Your protections come from your contract and general contract law.

This makes it even more important that your agreements are solid from the start. In Florida, your contract is your primary enforcement tool.

Note: If your client is based in a state that does have freelancer protection laws — such as New York or Illinois — those laws may apply to you even if you're located in Florida, depending on where the work was performed or where the client operates. Worth checking if you're dealing with an out-of-state client.

How to Make Late Fees Enforceable in Florida

Florida courts will enforce late fees if they're properly documented. Here's how to set things up correctly:

  • Put it in your contract. Include a clear clause: "Invoices unpaid after [X] days will accrue interest at 1.5% per month on the outstanding balance." Vague language like "late fees may apply" is not enough.
  • Repeat it on every invoice. List payment terms, due date, and late fee rate directly on the invoice. This ensures the client saw the terms at the time of billing.
  • Send a written reminder before applying fees. An email or written notice creates a paper trail and strengthens your position if you ever need to pursue the matter further.
  • Issue an updated invoice when fees kick in. Show the original amount, the accrued late fee, and the new total. Keep this clean and professional.

Collecting Overdue Invoices in Florida

If a client refuses to pay after multiple attempts, Florida Small Claims Court handles disputes up to $8,000. Filing costs are low — typically $30–$100 depending on the amount — and you don't need a lawyer to represent yourself.

For larger amounts, County Court handles civil cases up to $50,000 (the limit rose from $30,000 on January 1, 2023), and Circuit Court takes anything above that. At these levels, an attorney may be worth the cost — and if your contract has an attorney's fee clause, Fla. Stat. § 57.105(7) makes it work both ways, so whoever wins can recover fees.

  • Bring your contract, all invoices, and every written communication about the debt
  • Show you made reasonable attempts to collect before going to court
  • Document when the work was completed and when payment was due

Florida also allows you to place a lien on a client's property in certain situations — particularly in construction and service industries. If your work involves improvements to real property, look into Florida's lien laws as an additional avenue.

Two Cases These Rules Don't Cover

Government agencies. If your client is a Florida state or local agency, your invoice terms generally don't set the late payment interest — a prompt payment statute does, with its own deadlines and its own rate. Writing a higher rate into your own terms doesn't override it, and the statutory interest you're already owed is easy to overlook. Look up the specific statute for the agency you're invoicing rather than relying on your contract language.

Daily late fees. Nothing on this page blesses a fee stated as a percentage per day. Computing an annual rate daily is normal; writing "2% per day" into your terms is a different thing, and it annualizes to 730% — above any ceiling and vulnerable as an unenforceable penalty. See is a daily late fee legal on an invoice for what courts do with them. These rules also aren't freelancer-specific: they're Florida's general commercial invoicing rules, and they apply the same way to a contractor, vendor or supplier billing another business.

Practical Tips for Florida Freelancers

  • Always use a written contract — verbal agreements are nearly impossible to enforce
  • State your late fee rate in at least two places: the contract and the invoice
  • Send invoices promptly after completing work — delays on your end weaken your position
  • Follow up in writing (email is fine) so you have a timestamped record
  • Don't let invoices go past 90 days without escalating — older debts are harder to collect
  • Be willing to negotiate. 80% of what you're owed is better than a prolonged dispute

Florida's rules are workable for freelancers — 18% simple interest plus a modest flat charge is enough leverage for most invoices — but only if you've documented everything properly. Set your terms upfront, put them in writing, and you'll have a solid foundation to collect what you're owed.

What late fee rate is enforceable in Florida? +

Up to 1.5% per month (18% per year simple interest), stated in your contract and on your invoice before work begins. Florida's usury statute, Fla. How does Florida's small claims limit compare to Georgia? +

Florida's small claims court caps disputes at $8,000, nearly half of neighboring Georgia's $15,000 limit. A Florida freelancer with a larger unpaid invoice may need county court instead, while the same dollar amount could often stay in small claims across the Georgia border.

Can I charge a 5% late fee on invoices in Florida? +

Generally yes, if your contract or signed terms say so before the work starts. A one-time 5% late charge covers the cost of chasing payment and is separate from interest, which Florida caps at 18% per year simple (Fla. Stat. § 687.03). Keep the flat charge proportionate to the invoice and charge it once, not every month.

Can I charge a monthly late fee on invoices in Florida? +

Yes, as interest: 1.5% per month on the unpaid balance is the maximum that stays within Florida's 18%-per-year simple-interest ceiling. Calculate it on the original balance rather than compounding, because 1.5% compounded monthly works out to about 19.6% a year. A repeating flat fee every month is harder to defend than interest.
