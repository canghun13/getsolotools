Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in Connecticut

September 22, 2026 · 7 min read

Connecticut looks strict on paper — a 12% usury cap that carries criminal penalties. But that statute is written for loans, and a freelance invoice for services isn't one. Connecticut also has something most states don't: a statute that lets a court award interest on an unpaid invoice even if your contract never mentioned a late fee. Here's what CT freelancers need to know.

The 12% Usury Cap Is Written for Loans — Not Your Invoice

Connecticut's usury statute, Conn. Gen. Stat. § 37-4, prohibits interest above 12% a year, and charging more on a covered loan can be prosecuted as a crime. That's the number most Connecticut freelancers have heard, and it's why many assume the national-standard 18% (1.5%/month) is off limits.

The statute's own language is about loans of money, though, and Connecticut courts have read it that way. Case annotations to § 37-4 record that the statute does not apply to sales on credit (3 Conn. App. 306) and does not apply where the debt arose from the purchase of services rather than from a loan of money (53 Conn. App. 455). A freelancer invoicing for delivered work isn't lending the client money — so the 12% ceiling is a much weaker constraint on a services invoice than it first appears.

That said, the protection comes from case law rather than a clean statutory exemption, and case law turns on facts. The pragmatic answer is still to write a rate at or below 1% per month (12% a year) into your contract. You give up very little compared with 1.5%, and you remove the argument entirely. If a late fee stated per day is what you're weighing, that's a separate problem — see is a daily late fee legal on an invoice.

You Can Get Interest Even Without a Late Fee Clause

This is the Connecticut feature most freelancers don't know about. Under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 37-3a, a court may award interest of up to 10% a year as damages for the detention of money after it becomes payable. It applies to obligations other than a loan of money — which describes an unpaid invoice precisely.

It's discretionary, but the bar is lower than it sounds. A court decides whether an award is fair and equitable, rather than granting it automatically. But the Connecticut Supreme Court held in DiLieto v. County Obstetrics & Gynecology Group that a wrongful detention of money is established simply by winning on the underlying claim — you don't have to separately prove the client acted in bad faith.

Separately, § 37-1 sets 8% a year as the legal rate that accrues on a matured debt when no rate was agreed. A written late fee clause is still better — it's certain rather than discretionary, and it starts accruing without a lawsuit — but if you're chasing an old invoice from a contract that never mentioned interest, Connecticut gives you more to work with than most states.

Connecticut's $5,000 Small Claims Limit Is Low

Connecticut small claims tops out at $5,000 for most disputes under Conn. Gen. Stat. § 51-15(d). The higher $15,000 ceiling applies only to home improvement contracts with registered contractors and new home construction contracts — not to general freelance work. The filing fee is $95, and in routine cases the court handles service by mail, so there's usually no separate sheriff's fee.

That ceiling is on the low side nationally, which matters for project sizing. A $9,000 unpaid invoice is a straightforward self-filed claim in many states, but in Connecticut it goes to the regular Superior Court civil docket. If you regularly bill Connecticut clients more than $5,000 at a time, that's a strong argument for milestone billing — see how many payment milestones a project needs — so that no single unpaid invoice outgrows small claims.

Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in Connecticut

  • Write a rate at or below 1% per month (12% a year) into every contract. The services carve-out likely gives you room above that, but 12% removes the question.
  • State the rate on each invoice, not just in the original agreement, so it's visible when payment becomes overdue.
  • Keep individual invoices at or under $5,000 where you can, so an unpaid one stays in small claims.
  • Remember § 37-3a if you have no clause. Ask for interest as damages for detention of money when you file.
  • File within 6 years for written contracts (Conn. Gen. Stat. § 52-576).

Collecting in Connecticut

Small claims cases are filed in the Superior Court's small claims session for the area where the defendant lives or does business.

  • Small claims: up to $5,000 (§ 51-15(d)); $15,000 only for home improvement and new home construction contracts
  • Filing fee: $95; court-handled mail service in routine cases
  • Written contract statute of limitations: 6 years (§ 52-576)
  • Interest: up to 10% a year as damages for detention of money (§ 37-3a), discretionary; 8% legal rate on matured debts with no agreed rate (§ 37-1)
