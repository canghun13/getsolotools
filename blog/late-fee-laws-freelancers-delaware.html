The Problem: Delaware's Rate Cap Moves With the Fed — But Corporate Clients Can't Use It Against You

Delaware doesn't set a fixed usury ceiling. Under 6 Del. C. §2301, the legal rate of interest — both the default rate when a contract is silent and the general cap on written agreements — is 5 percentage points over the Federal Reserve discount rate, including any surcharge. With the discount rate near 3.75% as of mid-2026, that works out to roughly 8.75% per year, well under the 18% (1.5%/month) most freelancers use nationally. Loans over $100,000 not secured by a primary residence carry no cap at all under §2301(c), but that threshold is out of reach for most individual freelance invoices.

Here's the genuinely useful part: 6 Del. C. §2306 states that no corporation, LLC, business trust, or similar entity may ever raise usury as a legal defense in any action — a rule tied to Delaware's reputation as the country's default incorporation state. Since most freelance clients are set up as an LLC or corporation rather than billing you as an individual, a documented late fee rate above the floating 8.75% cap is very difficult for that entity to successfully dispute on usury grounds. Freelancers should still state the rate clearly in writing and, when billing an actual individual consumer rather than a business entity, lean toward the more conservative floating legal rate instead.

Delaware's $25,000 Small Claims Limit Is the Highest in the Country

Delaware's Justice of the Peace Court — the state's lower trial court, which doubles as its small claims venue — hears civil disputes up to $25,000 under 10 Del. C. §9301, a figure multiple independent sources confirm as the highest small-claims jurisdictional ceiling of any U.S. state. Filing fees range from roughly $35 to $120 depending on the claim amount, plus a small per-document technology fee. One notable wrinkle: unlike most small claims courts designed for self-represented parties, corporations appearing in Delaware's Justice of the Peace Court must be represented by counsel — they can't appear pro se the way an individual freelancer can.

The tradeoff shows up in filing deadlines: Delaware's 3-year statute of limitations for written contracts matches Maryland's short window but is a third of West Virginia's generous 10-year period one state over, so Delaware freelancers should be more prompt about pursuing unpaid invoices than freelancers in several neighboring states.

Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in Delaware

  • Write your rate into every contract and confirm whether your client is a registered business entity — this determines whether Delaware's corporate usury-defense bar (§2306) applies to your specific relationship.
  • State the rate on the invoice itself, not just in the original agreement, so it's visible when payment becomes overdue.
  • Know your court options. For invoices up to $25,000, Delaware's Justice of the Peace Court is the fastest, most accessible path — the highest small claims ceiling in the country.
  • File within 3 years for written contracts (10 Del. C. §8106) — shorter than many states in this series, so don't sit on an unpaid invoice.

Large contracts can extend Delaware's filing deadline dramatically. Delaware's standard 3-year statute of limitations is short by national standards, but 10 Del. C. §8106(c) allows written contracts of $100,000 or more to contractually extend the filing window up to 20 years, with clear language in the agreement. This mostly benefits larger engagements rather than typical freelance invoices, but it's a distinctive feature of Delaware's business-friendly contract law worth knowing if you ever negotiate a large, multi-year retainer.

Collecting in Delaware

Delaware's Justice of the Peace Court hears civil disputes up to $25,000 (10 Del. C. §9301), filed in the county where the defendant resides or where the obligation arose.

  • Delaware Justice of the Peace Court: up to $25,000, filing fees roughly $35–$120 plus a small technology fee
  • Delaware Superior Court: for claims above $25,000
  • Written contract statute of limitations: 3 years (10 Del. C. §8106); extendable to 20 years for contracts of $100,000+ with explicit language
  • Post-judgment interest: 5% over the Federal Reserve discount rate, or the contract rate, whichever is lower
