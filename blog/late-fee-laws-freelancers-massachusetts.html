Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in Massachusetts

July 16, 2026 · Updated Nov 9, 2026 · 7 min read

Massachusetts has one of the highest statutory contract interest rates in the country at 12% annually, and gives freelancers a full six years to sue over an unpaid invoice — among the longest windows in the U.S. There's no dedicated freelancer protection act, but the state's consumer protection law has a business-to-business provision, and its strict contractor test can turn a misclassified freelancer's unpaid invoice into triple damages. Here's what Massachusetts freelancers need to know about charging and collecting late fees.

Massachusetts' 12% Contract Interest Rate

Under Massachusetts General Laws c. 231, § 6C, a court adds interest to damages for breach of contract at the contract rate, if one was established, or 12% per year if it wasn't — calculated from the date of breach or demand, or from when the lawsuit was filed if no earlier date is established. The same 12% rate applies to post-judgment interest under M.G.L. c. 235, § 8. The 12% fallback is significantly higher than the 6-8% default rate most states use, and it applies even if your contract never mentions interest. If your contract does set a rate, the court uses your rate instead — so a clause below 12% a year can actually leave money on the table in Massachusetts.

That said, 12% is the rate a court applies if you have to sue. It doesn't set what you can charge in your own late fee clause. Most Massachusetts freelancers write 1.5% per month (18% annually) into their contracts — under the 20%-a-year criminal usury line in M.G.L. c. 271, § 49 and standard enough that clients rarely push back. Note that 2% per month is 24% a year, which is over that line; there's no reason to go there when the 12% statutory fallback already exists.

Can You Charge a Flat Late Fee in Massachusetts?

Yes, for business clients, if the contract says so before the work starts. Massachusetts has no statute setting a maximum flat late fee on a business-to-business service invoice; a court will ask whether the amount is a reasonable estimate of what late payment costs you, not a penalty. A one-time charge such as $25 or 5% of the invoice is easy to defend; a flat fee that repeats every month starts to look like a penalty. You can pair a modest flat fee with monthly interest — see charging a flat late fee and interest together. (Consumer and residential rules are separate and stricter.)

Six Years to Collect

Massachusetts gives you six years from the date of breach to sue on a written or oral contract (M.G.L. c. 260, § 2) — longer than most states, where the window is often 3 to 4 years. Practically, this means an old unpaid invoice you'd written off elsewhere may still be collectible in Massachusetts. It's not a reason to delay collection, but it's useful to know if a client relationship went sideways months ago and you're only now deciding to pursue it.

Chapter 93A: Massachusetts' Unfair Business Practices Law

Massachusetts' Consumer Protection Act, M.G.L. c. 93A, is best known for protecting consumers, but § 11 of the same chapter extends similar protection to businesses in disputes with other businesses. If a client's non-payment crosses the line from "slow" into a genuinely unfair or deceptive business practice — for example, deliberately stringing you along with false promises to pay, or withholding payment for delivered work as leverage to extract a discount — a Chapter 93A claim can add attorney's fees and, for a willful or knowing violation, double or triple damages. A simple breach of contract, on its own, is generally not enough.

Good to know, not a first move: The 30-day demand letter in § 9 is for consumer claims; a business-to-business claim under § 11 doesn't require one, though sending a firm written demand is still smart. Either way, 93A is a heavier tool than most freelance payment disputes need. For a straightforward late payment, your late fee clause and small claims court are usually faster and simpler. Save Chapter 93A for situations that look like bad-faith business conduct, not just a slow-paying client.

If You're Really an Employee: The Wage Act's Treble Damages

Massachusetts has no freelancer protection act, but it has one of the strictest independent contractor tests in the country. Under M.G.L. c. 149, § 148B, a worker is presumed to be an employee unless the hiring business proves all three parts of an "ABC" test — including that the work is outside the usual course of the hiring company's business. If a client controls your schedule and you're doing the same work its staff does, you may legally be an employee. Unpaid wages owed to an employee carry mandatory triple damages plus attorney's fees under c. 149, § 150. This only helps if you genuinely fail the contractor test — but if you do, it's far stronger leverage than any late fee clause.

Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in Massachusetts

Massachusetts courts enforce late fee clauses the same way most states do — as long as the terms are clear, agreed to before work begins, and not punitive on their face:

  • Put the rate in writing. "Invoices unpaid after 30 days accrue interest at 1.5% per month on the outstanding balance" is specific enough to enforce.
  • Repeat it on every invoice. Clients' accounts payable teams often work from the invoice alone, not the underlying contract.
  • Stay under 20% a year. 1.5% per month (18%) is standard commercial practice and under Massachusetts' 20% criminal usury threshold; 2% per month (24%) is not.
  • Send a written notice before escalating. A polite reminder that references your contract terms protects you if the dispute ever reaches small claims court.

Collecting in Massachusetts

Massachusetts Small Claims Session, part of the District Court, handles disputes up to $7,000 (M.G.L. c. 218, § 21) — with no cap for property damage from a motor vehicle. It's designed for self-represented filers: no attorney required, filing fees range from $40 to $150 depending on the amount claimed, and the clerk-magistrate handles service of the complaint for you.

  • Massachusetts Small Claims Session: up to $7,000 — no attorney required, low filing fee
  • Massachusetts District Court civil docket: for claims above $7,000
  • Chapter 93A demand letter: for cases involving bad-faith or deceptive non-payment, not routine late payment

What late fee rate is enforceable in Massachusetts? +

If your contract sets no rate, Massachusetts courts add 12% annual interest to a contract judgment (M.G.L. c. 231, § 6C); if it does, they use your rate. Most freelancers write 1.5% per month (18% annually), which stays under the state's 20%-a-year criminal usury threshold (c. 271, § 49). 2% per month (24%) would cross it.

How long do I have to sue over an unpaid invoice in Massachusetts? +

Massachusetts allows six years from the date of breach to sue on a written or oral contract (M.G.L. c. 260, § 2) — longer than most states.

How does Massachusetts's small claims limit compare to New York? +

Massachusetts small claims court caps disputes at $7,000, lower than New York City's $10,000 limit, even though Massachusetts has the higher statutory interest rate (12% a year versus New York's 9% under CPLR 5004). A larger unpaid invoice may clear small claims in NYC but not in Massachusetts.

Can you charge a flat late fee in Massachusetts for services? +

Yes, for business clients, if your contract or signed terms provide for it before the work starts. Massachusetts has no statute capping a flat late fee on a B2B service invoice, but a court will reject an amount that works as a penalty. A modest one-time fee such as $25 or 5% of the invoice is easier to defend than a fee that repeats every month.

What is the legal maximum late fee percentage in Massachusetts for an invoice? +

No statute sets a late-fee percentage for business invoices. The practical ceiling for interest is the 20%-a-year criminal usury line in M.G.L. c. 271, § 49, so 1.5% per month (18% a year) is the common written rate. If your contract is silent, a court adds 12% a year under c. 231, § 6C.
