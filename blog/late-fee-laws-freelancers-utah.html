Late Fee Laws for Freelancers in Utah

September 22, 2026 · 7 min read

Utah is one of the most freelancer-friendly states in this series on late fees, and it's unusually explicit about it: since 2019 the interest statute names contracts for services directly. There's no usury ceiling on a rate you agree to, a 10% default if you didn't, and one of the higher small claims limits in the country — on a schedule that rises again in 2030. Here's what Utah freelancers need to know.

Utah Lets You Agree Any Rate — and Says So for Service Contracts

Under Utah Code § 15-1-1(1), the parties to a lawful contract may agree on any rate of interest. What makes Utah distinctive is the wording added in the 2019 amendment: the statute now expressly covers written, verbal, or implied contracts, including a contract for services, as well as claims for breach of contract. Many states leave freelancers to argue by analogy from loan-focused usury statutes. Utah removes the question.

So the national-standard 1.5% per month (18% a year) is on solid ground in Utah if it's in your agreement. "Any rate" isn't a licence for anything, though. A charge large enough to read as punishment rather than compensation can still be struck down as an unenforceable penalty — which is where very aggressive clauses, particularly fees stated per day, run into trouble. See is a daily late fee legal on an invoice for how that test works.

No Rate in the Contract? The Default Is 10% a Year — Not a Month

If your contract doesn't specify a rate, § 15-1-1(2) sets the legal rate at 10% per annum, and the 2019 language makes clear that applies to service contracts and breach-of-contract claims too. That's more generous than the 5–8% default many states use.

Per annum means per year. It sounds obvious, but the confusion shows up in real disputes — for example, a contractor claiming "10% monthly" on a lien where the contract named no rate at all. Ten percent a month is 120% a year, twelve times what the statute allows as a default.

If you're on the receiving end of that kind of claim, the statute is your answer. If you're the one billing, and you want more than 10%, write the rate into the contract — Utah will enforce it.

Utah's Small Claims Limit Is $20,000 — and Rising

Utah is on a statutory escalator. Under Utah Code § 78A-8-102, the small claims ceiling is $20,000 for cases filed from January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2029, and it rises automatically to $25,000 on January 1, 2030. The limit includes attorney fees but excludes court costs and interest.

Watch for stale figures: some older sources and city court websites still show $15,000, which only applied through the end of 2024. Small claims cases are heard in Justice Court in the city or county where the defendant lives or where the claim arose, and — unlike several states — Utah allows attorneys to appear in small claims.

At $20,000, most individual freelance invoices fit comfortably inside small claims in Utah. That changes the arithmetic on whether chasing an unpaid invoice is worth it; is it worth suing a client over an unpaid invoice works through it against your own time.

Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in Utah

  • Write your rate into the contract if you want more than the 10% default. Utah will enforce an agreed rate on a services contract.
  • State the rate as monthly or annual, not daily. An agreed rate still has to survive the penalty test.
  • Put the rate on each invoice, not just in the original agreement.
  • File within 6 years for written contracts (Utah Code § 78B-2-309) — or 4 years for oral contracts (§ 78B-2-307), another reason to get the engagement in writing.

Collecting in Utah

  • Small claims (Justice Court): up to $20,000 through 2029, $25,000 from January 1, 2030 (§ 78A-8-102)
  • Written contract statute of limitations: 6 years (§ 78B-2-309); oral contracts: 4 years (§ 78B-2-307)
  • Default interest with no agreed rate: 10% a year (§ 15-1-1(2))
  • Post-judgment interest: the federal post-judgment rate as of January 1 plus 2% (§ 15-1-4), or the contract rate where the judgment is based on a contract that fixes one
