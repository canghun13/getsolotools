Late Fee Laws for Freelancers & Small Businesses in New Hampshire

October 12, 2026 · 7 min read

New Hampshire gives business invoices a 10% default rate, lets a written business agreement set any rate — even compounding — and runs small claims up to $10,000. The catch is time: you have just three years to sue on an unpaid invoice, shorter than most states. Here's what New Hampshire freelancers and small businesses need to know.

No Rate in the Contract? New Hampshire Gives Business Invoices 10%

Under RSA 336:1, I, the rate of interest on a business transaction is 10% a year unless the parties agreed otherwise in writing. Consumer credit transactions are carved out, so the 10% applies to invoices between businesses — which covers a freelancer billing a company. That's higher than Connecticut's 8% default, and in Maine an unpaid invoice gets no statutory rate at all until you serve a notice of claim.

Once a case is filed, a different clock takes over: prejudgment and judgment interest run at a floating rate set by the state treasurer each year — the 26-week Treasury bill rate plus 2 points (RSA 336:1, II). In a year when T-bills are near 4%, that's roughly 6%, which is lower than the 10% business rate. Another reason to write your own rate into the contract.

Is There a Cap on a Written Late Fee in New Hampshire?

Not for business transactions. RSA 336:1 doesn't just let you agree a different rate in writing — it expressly lets a written agreement on a business transaction provide for charges other than simple interest. That's unusual: in most states, compounding or a stacked fee structure is where trouble starts. In New Hampshire, a written business agreement can provide for it.

What that does and doesn't mean. The national-standard 1.5% a month (18% a year) is on solid ground with a business client. But "permitted by statute" isn't the same as "any number goes" — a charge that can only be read as punishment, especially one stated per day, can still be struck as a penalty. See is a daily late fee legal on an invoice, and if you're combining a flat fee with interest, can you charge a flat late fee and interest on the same invoice.

For individual clients buying for personal or household use, consumer credit rules apply instead — keep those clauses simple and modest.

Three Years to Sue — Shorter Than Most States

RSA 508:4 gives three years for most personal actions, including breach of contract, measured from the act or omission complained of. Most states give five or six years on a written contract. In New Hampshire, an invoice that went unpaid in early 2024 is close to the line by early 2027. If a client has stopped responding, decide early whether you'll pursue it.

New Hampshire Small Claims: $10,000

Small claims in New Hampshire is part of the District Division of the Circuit Court, with a ceiling of $10,000 excluding interest and costs (RSA 503:1). The District Division hears other civil cases up to $25,000; above that is Superior Court. Lawyers are allowed but not required, and most people file on their own. Setting Up Enforceable Late Fees in New Hampshire

  • Write the rate into the contract — 1.5% a month is standard and uncapped for business transactions.
  • If you want compounding or a combined flat fee + interest, say so in writing. RSA 336:1 permits it for business transactions, but only by written agreement.
  • Put the rate and due date on every invoice, not only in the original agreement.
  • Watch the three-year clock. Calendar a decision date for every invoice more than six months overdue.

Collecting in New Hampshire

  • Small claims (Circuit Court, District Division): up to $10,000, excluding interest and costs (RSA 503:1)
  • District Division civil cases: up to $25,000; Superior Court above that
  • Default interest on a business transaction with no written rate: 10% a year (RSA 336:1, I)
  • Judgment and prejudgment interest: 26-week Treasury bill rate + 2 points, reset annually (RSA 336:1, II)
  • Statute of limitations: 3 years for most personal actions, including contract (RSA 508:4)
