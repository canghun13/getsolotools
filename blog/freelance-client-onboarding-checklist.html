Freelance Client Onboarding Checklist: How to Start Every Project Right

May 10, 2026 · Updated Nov 16, 2026 · 8 min read

How you start a project determines how it ends. Freelancers who skip onboarding steps find themselves chasing unclear briefs, dealing with scope creep, and waiting on late payments. A consistent onboarding process prevents all of it.

Here's the checklist to run through before starting any new client engagement.

Before You Start Work

Agreements & Admin

  • Contract signed by both parties
  • Deposit invoice sent and payment received
  • Project scope confirmed in writing
  • Timeline and key milestones agreed
  • Revision policy explained and acknowledged
  • Payment schedule confirmed

Project Setup

  • Primary point of contact identified (who approves work?)
  • Communication channel agreed (email, Slack, etc.)
  • Feedback timeline set ("I'll need feedback within X business days")
  • Project management tool or shared folder set up
  • Access to required accounts, assets, or logins received

Brief & Brand Assets

  • Creative brief or project brief received
  • Brand guidelines received (if applicable)
  • Existing assets received (logos, fonts, copy, photos)
  • Examples of what the client likes / doesn't like
  • Target audience defined

Why Each Step Matters

Contract + deposit first. Never start work without both. The contract protects you legally; the deposit confirms the client is serious. Clients who won't sign a contract or pay a deposit before work begins are clients who will cause problems later.

Identify the decision-maker. On larger projects, you might be talking to a project coordinator but need sign-off from a director. Find out early who has final approval authority. Feedback from someone who can't actually approve work causes endless revision loops.

Set feedback timelines upfront. Tell the client how quickly you'll need their responses to stay on schedule. Put it in writing. When their delay causes a project to run late, you'll have documentation showing why.

Get all assets before starting. Starting design work before you have the logo, or writing copy before you have the brand guidelines, means you'll have to redo work. Ask for everything upfront, even if you don't need it immediately.

Send a Welcome Email

Once everything above is in place, send a short welcome email summarizing:

  • The project scope you'll be delivering
  • The timeline and first milestone
  • How to reach you and when you're available
  • What you need from them and when

This email takes 5 minutes to write and prevents 90% of project miscommunications. It also gives clients confidence that they've hired a professional.

The Kickoff Call: Setting the Tone for the Whole Project

Note this is the call after the contract is signed. The earlier call — before you quote, where budget, scope and payment terms are actually decided — is a different conversation with different questions: see client discovery call questions that decide your rate, scope and payment terms.

Once the contract is signed and the deposit is paid, a kickoff call is one of the most valuable things you can do before starting work. Even 20 to 30 minutes on a call can surface misalignments in expectations, fill gaps in the brief, and establish the working relationship on the right footing.

Come prepared with specific questions: What does success look like for this project? Who are the end users or intended audience? What have you tried before that didn't work? Who needs to approve deliverables and what's their timeline? Are there any constraints — brand guidelines, technical limitations, legal considerations — I should know about?

Summarize the key points in a follow-up email after the call. "Just to confirm what we discussed..." gives both parties a written record and catches any misunderstandings before they become problems mid-project.

Setting Communication Expectations Upfront

One of the biggest sources of client friction is mismatched communication expectations. A client who expects same-day responses and a freelancer who checks email twice a day will clash — and the freelancer usually bears the blame, even if their response time is perfectly reasonable.

Set your communication norms at the start: which channel you'll use (email, Slack, a project management tool), your typical response time, and when you're available. "I'm available Monday through Friday, 9am to 5pm, and I respond to emails within 24 hours" is a simple, professional statement that prevents most friction. Build these norms into your onboarding process so every new client hears them, not just the ones who ask.

Handling Scope Creep from Day One

Scope creep — additional work that wasn't in the original agreement — is one of the most common ways freelancers lose time and money. The best time to address it is before it happens, during onboarding. Make sure your contract explicitly defines what's included and what isn't, and establish a process for handling requests that fall outside the agreed scope.

When a client asks for something outside scope, don't just do it as a favor. Acknowledge the request, note that it falls outside the current project, and provide a brief estimate for adding it. This keeps the relationship professional, protects your time, and often leads to additional paid work rather than free work that breeds resentment.

Confirming the Rate Before Work Begins

Even if you've discussed pricing during the proposal stage, confirm the agreed rate explicitly as part of onboarding. This means referencing it in your contract, on your first invoice, and in your kickoff email. Ambiguity about rates is a common source of disputes — removing it entirely at the start of the engagement protects both sides.

If a client tries to renegotiate pricing after the contract is signed, hold firm. The time for rate discussion is before the contract, not after. Onboarding is also the right time to gauge whether a client could be a good fit for ongoing work. If the scope naturally repeats month to month, it's worth raising the idea of a retainer arrangement early, rather than re-negotiating a new project every time.

Onboarding Questions to Ask Every New Client

The checklist above tells you what to collect. These are the questions that get it, grouped by the problem each one prevents:

  • Approvals: "Who signs off on each deliverable — and is that the same person who approves my invoices?"
  • Feedback: "How many business days do you need to review a draft? Who consolidates feedback if several people comment?"
  • Assets and access: "Which files, logins and brand materials can you send this week, and who do I ask if something is missing?"
  • Scope edges: "Is there anything you're expecting that isn't in the scope document?" — asked out loud, this surfaces scope creep before it starts.
  • Communication: "Where do you want updates — email, Slack, your project tool — and how often?"
  • Getting paid: "Where should invoices go, and is there anything your accounts payable team needs from me first?"

The Accounts-Payable Checklist Most Freelancers Skip

The most common reason a first invoice is late isn't an unhappy client — it's a missing vendor setup step nobody mentioned. Ask for these during onboarding, not when the invoice is already overdue:

Before you send the first invoice

  • Vendor or supplier registration completed (some companies need this before they can pay you at all)
  • W-9 sent if the client is a U.S. business (needed for their 1099 reporting)
  • Purchase order (PO) number issued, if they use them — invoices without one are often rejected
  • Invoice destination confirmed: an AP inbox, a portal (Coupa, Ariba, Bill.com), or a named person
  • Payment terms confirmed in writing (Net 15, Net 30) and matching your contract
  • Payment method set up — ACH details, card, or check — and any fees agreed
  • Name of the person to contact if an invoice is stuck in approval

Discipline-Specific Additions

The core checklist works for any freelancer. Add these lines depending on what you do:

Graphic and brand designers

  • Creative brief with the problem to solve, not just the deliverable
  • Examples of designs the client likes and dislikes, with a reason for each
  • Final file formats and where the files will be used (print, web, social)
  • Number of concepts and revision rounds, stated per deliverable
  • Who owns fonts, stock images and licences after handover

Web developers

  • Hosting, domain registrar and DNS access (or who controls them)
  • Staging environment and deployment process
  • Browser and device support list
  • Who is responsible for content entry, copy and images
  • Maintenance and bug-fix window after launch

Writers and content creators

  • Brand voice guide or three examples of on-brand writing
  • SEO keywords or briefs, if any, and who supplies them
  • Approval chain, including legal or compliance review
  • Byline, ghostwriting and reuse rights
  • Kill fee terms if a piece is cancelled — see the kill fee calculator

Social media managers (including your first client)

  • Partner or role-based access through the platform's business tools — not shared passwords
  • Content approval process and a posting calendar both sides can see
  • Who replies to comments and direct messages, and how fast
  • Brand voice, visual guidelines and topics to avoid
  • Which metrics you report, how often, and the baseline numbers on day one

Frequently Asked Questions

What questions should I ask a new client during onboarding? +

Who approves the work and who approves invoices, how fast they can give feedback, which assets and logins you'll get and by when, how they want to communicate, and how invoices get paid — PO number, vendor forms, invoice email or portal, and payment terms.

What should a freelancer onboarding checklist include? +

A signed contract and paid deposit, written scope and revision policy, a named decision-maker, an agreed feedback turnaround, all assets and access, a communication channel and response time, and the client's accounts-payable details: vendor setup, W-9, PO number and where invoices go.

What should I check before taking my first social media client? +

Get partner or role-based access to their accounts rather than passwords, agree an approval process and posting calendar, confirm who replies to comments and messages, collect brand voice and visual guidelines, and agree which metrics you'll report and how often.
