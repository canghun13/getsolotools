Can You Charge a Flat Late Fee and Interest on the Same Invoice?

October 5, 2026 · 8 min read

Most freelancers pick one: a percentage that accrues each month, or a flat fee when a payment is late. Plenty of contracts use both — "$35 late fee plus 1.5% per month" — and the questions that follow are always the same. Is that double-dipping? Can the flat fee repeat every month? Does it count against my state's interest cap? The answer turns on one idea courts keep coming back to: each charge has to compensate a different loss.

Two Charges, Two Different Losses

When a client pays late, you lose two separate things:

  • The use of the money for the time it was late. That is what interest compensates, which is why it is expressed as a rate over time and grows the longer the invoice sits.
  • The time you spend chasing it — reminders, a second invoice, a phone call, sometimes a call to the bookkeeper. That cost is roughly the same whether the payment is ten days late or ninety, which is why a fixed fee fits it.

As long as each charge maps to its own loss, charging both is generally fine when the contract says so. A Florida collections attorney puts it plainly for that state: a 5% late charge can be collected in addition to 18% interest when the contract provides for both. Trouble starts when the two charges stop being distinct.

When the Flat Fee Gets Struck: The Dobson Bay Case

The clearest example is Dobson Bay Club II DD, LLC v. La Sonrisa de Siena, LLC, decided by the Arizona Supreme Court in 2017. A borrower missed a balloon payment on a $28.6 million commercial loan. The contract charged default interest and a 5% late fee on the balance. The court:

  • Enforced the default interest, because it compensated the lender for losing the use of the money.
  • Refused to enforce the 5% late fee, because it duplicated what the interest already covered and had no connection to how long the payment was late.

The test it applied — whether the fee was a reasonable forecast of the actual loss (Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356) — is the same liquidated-damages test most states use. The lesson for a freelance invoice: a flat fee that's a large percentage of the balance, sitting next to a monthly rate on the same balance, is the version most exposed. A flat fee sized to your real admin cost is not.

When a Flat Fee Is Not Interest: The Texas Cable Case

This matters most in states with a rate ceiling. In Garcia v. Texas Cable Partners (Tex. App. 2003), a customer argued that a cable company's $5 late fee was usurious interest. The court disagreed: the fee was the same for every customer, had no relationship to how much they owed, and was described as an advance estimate of the company's costs from late payments. Because it wasn't compensation for the use or forbearance of money, it wasn't interest.

So for Texas: 1.5% a month is exactly the 18% commercial line. A small, fixed, cost-based flat fee on top is the defensible add-on. A fee that scales with the invoice (a percentage) or repeats on the same unpaid balance every month starts behaving like interest — and in Texas there is no room left above 1.5% for it. Can the Flat Fee Repeat Every Month?

This is the question behind most "flat fee plus interest" searches, and it is where structures get into trouble. A one-time fee per missed payment looks like cost recovery. The same fee charged again every month on the same unpaid invoice grows with time — which is the defining feature of interest. In a state with a ceiling, that makes it likely to be added to your rate when a court checks it.

Some states write the one-time rule down. Mississippi's late-charge statute allows the charge "only one (1) time on a specific installment" (Miss. Code § 75-17-27) — see the Mississippi guide. Even where the law is silent, the safest drafting is: flat fee once, percentage monthly. If you want the late charge to keep growing, let the interest rate do it. On a retainer, a new flat fee for each new invoice that is paid late is a different thing — each one is a separate missed payment.

A Clause That Keeps the Two Separate

"If an invoice is not paid within 15 days of its due date, a one-time late fee of $[amount] applies to cover the cost of collection. In addition, unpaid balances accrue interest at [rate]% per month from the due date until paid, not to exceed the maximum rate permitted by law."

  • Size the flat fee to your real cost — an hour or two of your time chasing payment, not a percentage of the invoice.
  • Say "one-time" so nobody reads it as monthly.
  • Keep the rate inside your state's ceiling — 1.5% a month is common, but it's already at the line in Texas and over it in Mississippi.
  • The savings clause ("not to exceed the maximum rate permitted by law") isn't a cure-all, but it shows intent to stay within the limit.
  • Put both on every invoice, not only in the original contract.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I charge both a flat late fee and interest on an overdue invoice? +

Usually yes, if both are in the contract and each compensates a different loss. Interest compensates you for waiting for the money; a modest flat fee compensates the administrative cost of chasing the payment. Courts have upheld default interest while striking a flat fee that duplicated it: in Dobson Bay Club II v. La Sonrisa de Siena (Ariz. 2017) the Arizona Supreme Court enforced default interest but held a 5% late fee unenforceable because it covered the same loss and had nothing to do with how long the payment was late.

Can a flat late fee repeat every month the invoice stays unpaid? +

It is the riskiest part of the structure. A one-time fee per missed payment reads as cost recovery. The same flat fee charged again every month on the same unpaid balance grows with time, which is what interest does, so in a state with a rate ceiling it can be counted toward that ceiling. Mississippi's statute, for example, allows a late charge only once per installment. If you want the charge to grow with time, let the interest do it.

Texas allows 1.5% a month. Can I add a flat late fee on top? +

Texas treats 18% a year as the ceiling for these commercial charges, and 1.5% a month is exactly 18%, so there is no room left if a flat fee is counted as interest. A Texas appeals court held that a flat $5 administrative late fee, charged the same to every customer regardless of the balance, was not interest because it was a cost estimate rather than compensation for the use of money (Garcia v. Texas Cable Partners, 2003). A small, fixed, cost-based fee is the defensible version; a fee that scales with the balance or repeats on the same debt is the one that can push you over 18%.

Why can a utility or doctor's office charge a flat $10 late fee when my state allows 1.5% a month? +

They are different legal categories. A percentage charged over time is interest and is measured against interest-rate rules. A flat fee that does not depend on the balance or the length of delay is usually treated as reimbursement for the cost of handling a late account. Utilities and medical offices also operate under their own regulations and consumer rules. For a business-to-business invoice, the same logic lets you charge a modest flat fee alongside interest, as long as both are in the contract.

Is a 5% flat late fee legal? +

It depends on what else you charge and how large the invoice is. On a small invoice a 5% one-time fee may be a reasonable estimate of the cost of chasing it. But a 5% fee was struck down in Dobson Bay because the contract already charged default interest for the same delay and the fee did not change with how late the payment was. If you charge 5% flat, don't stack a high monthly rate on the same balance, and don't repeat the 5% every month.
