Sending an NDA Email Template

An NDA protects both parties when sensitive information — business strategies, unreleased products, client lists, proprietary processes — needs to be shared before or during a project. Framing it as mutual protection rather than distrust is key to keeping the conversation comfortable.

When to send Send the NDA before any substantive project discussion that involves confidential information. It should be one of the first documents in your onboarding sequence — before the scope of work and before the contract.

The Template

Email Template
Subject: NDA for [Project Name] - Please Review and Sign
Hi [Client Name], Before we get into the details of [project name], I'd like to put a mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement in place to protect both of our interests. The NDA is attached. It's straightforward and covers: - Confidentiality of information shared by both parties - What constitutes confidential information for this project - The duration of the agreement Please review it and, if you're happy with the terms, sign and return it at your convenience. Once that's done, I'm ready to move forward with a full briefing. Let me know if you have any questions about any of the clauses. Best, [Your Name]

NDA Emails for Other Situations

The template above is for a freelancer sending their own NDA to a client. NDAs move in several other directions — after a demo, through a supplier portal, from a client whose template needs legal review, and back with redlines. These five cover them.

Sending an NDA after a meeting or demo, before sharing a deck
Subject: NDA Before We Share [Deck / Details] – [Your Company] & [Their Company]
Hi [Name], Thank you for your time [yesterday / at the demo this morning] — I hope it was useful. Before I send over [the deck / pricing / technical details] [Name] mentioned, could you review and sign the attached mutual NDA? It's a standard [two]-page agreement covering information we share during our evaluation, for [X] years. Once it's signed, I'll send the materials the same day. Best regards, [Your Name] [Title, Company]
Supplier NDA through a sourcing portal (Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, Ivalua) before an RFP
Subject: Action Required: NDA for [RFP / Event Name] in [Portal Name]
Dear [Supplier Contact], [Company] is preparing a request for proposal for [scope], and we'd like to include [Supplier Name]. Before we can share the RFP documents, we need a signed non-disclosure agreement. You'll receive a separate notification from [Ariba / Coupa / Jaggaer / Ivalua] for event [ID]. Please: 1. Log in or register with the email address this message was sent to 2. Open the NDA task and review the agreement 3. Sign electronically by [date] Once the NDA is executed, you'll get access to the RFP documents in the same portal. If you have trouble accessing the event, or your legal team needs to discuss the NDA terms, reply to this email before [date]. Kind regards, [Your Name] [Title, Procurement / Sourcing]
Client sent their NDA — holding reply while it's reviewed
Subject: Re: NDA – Under Review, Response by [Date]
Hi [Name], Thank you for sending over your NDA. I'm having it reviewed [by my attorney / by our management team] and will come back to you by [date] with either a signed copy or any comments. In the meantime, I'm happy to keep discussing the project at a general level, and I'll hold off on anything that needs confidential detail until the NDA is in place. Best, [Your Name]
Returning an NDA with requested changes
Subject: NDA – Signed with Two Small Changes / Comments for Your Review
Hi [Name], Thanks for sending the NDA. I've reviewed it and attached a version with tracked changes. There are [two] edits: 1. Section [X]: [change, e.g. made the agreement mutual, since I'll also be sharing my process and pricing] 2. Section [Y]: [change, e.g. reduced the term from 10 years to 3 years after the project ends] Everything else is fine as written. If those work for you, I'll sign the clean version right away. Best, [Your Name]
Sending your NDA template and asking who will sign
Subject: Our NDA Template – Who Will Sign for [Company]?
Hello [Name], Attached is our standard NDA. Before I send it for signature, could you let me know who at [Company] is authorized to sign agreements, along with their title and email address? Once I have that, I'll send it through [e-signature tool] so it can be signed in a couple of minutes. Kind regards, [Your Name]

What's the Best Way to Send an NDA by Email?

  • Send a PDF or an e-signature link for signing, and a Word file only when you expect the other side to propose edits.
  • Say in one line what it covers and for how long — mutual or one-way, and the term — so the reader can judge it before opening it.
  • Hold back the confidential material until it's signed. Saying so in the email is also the easiest way to speed up the signature.
  • Ask who signs if you're emailing someone who may not have signing authority.
  • Keep the executed copy with the date and the names of both signatories; you'll need it if confidentiality is ever disputed.

Tips for Getting This Right

  • Frame the NDA as mutual — it protects the client's information as much as yours. Mutual NDAs are less confrontational than one-way agreements.
  • Keep the email short and matter-of-fact. An overly formal tone around an NDA can make clients unnecessarily anxious.
  • Send before any briefing call where sensitive information will be discussed — not after.
  • Use an e-signature tool to make signing fast and easy.
  • If a client pushes back on specific clauses, be willing to discuss. An NDA negotiation is not a red flag — it's due diligence.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Sending the NDA after you've already discussed sensitive information — the protection only works if it's in place before the disclosure.
  • Using a one-sided NDA that only protects the client — mutual NDAs are standard and more likely to be signed without pushback.
  • Using overly aggressive or complex legal language — a simple, readable NDA gets signed faster than a 10-page legal document.
  • Not following up if it doesn't get signed — an unsigned NDA provides no protection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I actually need an NDA for every project?

No — NDAs are most valuable when genuinely sensitive information is involved. For standard creative or development work where the client isn't sharing proprietary data, a solid contract with a confidentiality clause may be sufficient.

What's the difference between a mutual and a one-way NDA?

A mutual NDA protects both parties' confidential information. A one-way NDA protects only one party (usually the client). Mutual NDAs are more appropriate for freelance relationships since both parties may share sensitive information.

How long should an NDA last?

One to two years is typical for freelance projects. Some NDAs have no expiration date for certain types of confidential information, like trade secrets. The duration should reflect how long the information is likely to remain sensitive.

What if a client refuses to sign an NDA?

It depends on the reason. If they want to modify specific clauses, that's normal and worth discussing. If they refuse to sign any form of confidentiality agreement before sharing sensitive information, that's a red flag about how they approach professional relationships generally.

Can an NDA be effective before the freelancer's start date?

Yes, if the NDA says so. An NDA can set an effective date earlier than the signature date, or expressly cover information disclosed before signing — for example, during the pitch. If it doesn't say either, assume it only covers what's shared after it's signed.

How should I reply when a client asks me to sign their NDA?

Acknowledge it, say when you'll respond, and review it before signing. Check whether it's mutual, how long it lasts, whether it restricts your future work with other clients, and whether it includes non-solicitation or IP terms that belong in a different agreement.
